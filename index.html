Transfer Learning on the Kaggle Dogs vs. Cats Dataset

A compact case study fine-tuning a pretrained CNN on real photographs, with Grad-CAM visualization and error analysis.

Overview

This project is a compact computer vision case study, built on the Cats vs. Dogs dataset: roughly 25,000 labeled photographs of cats and dogs.

The approach is transfer learning: fine-tuning a backbone pretrained on a large image corpus rather than training a network from scratch. The images are real photographs, with varied lighting, pose, background, and occlusion. The goal isn’t to chase leaderboard accuracy; it’s to demonstrate a real, current computer vision workflow: standard augmentation, evaluation beyond accuracy, and Grad-CAM visualization of what the model is actually looking at.

Problem Statement

Given a photograph of a cat or a dog, the system must:

  1. Fine-tune a pretrained CNN to classify the image as cat or dog.
  2. Evaluate beyond accuracy: confusion matrix, per-class precision/recall/F1.
  3. Visualize model attention with Grad-CAM, and inspect the most confidently wrong predictions.

Methodology

  • An integrity pass that drops undecodable files, then a stratified 70/15/15 train/validation/test split of the labeled images.
  • Standard photographic augmentation: horizontal flip, rotation, color jitter, resize/crop to the backbone’s expected input size.
  • A pretrained ResNet18 backbone with its final classification head replaced for binary output.
  • Fine-tune with early layers frozen, then optionally unfreeze the last few blocks for a short additional phase.
  • Evaluate on the held-out test set, which neither training nor checkpoint selection ever sees: accuracy, precision, recall, F1, confusion matrix, Grad-CAM on a sample of correct and incorrect predictions, and error analysis on the most confidently wrong predictions.

Dataset

The project uses the Microsoft Cats vs. Dogs dataset, as distributed on Kaggle.

  • Dataset: https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/shaunthesheep/microsoft-catsvsdogs-dataset

Roughly 25,000 labeled photographs (cat vs. dog), real-world images with varied lighting, pose, background, and occlusion. They arrive as one folder per class with no predefined train/test division, so every split used here is produced in notebook 01 and written to disk for the later notebooks to read.

Status

Complete. All three notebooks have been run end to end and are published with their outputs.

Test accuracy: 98.82% on 3,744 held-out images that neither training nor checkpoint selection ever saw. Per-class F1 is 0.988 for both classes, and the errors are close to symmetric: 24 cats read as dogs against 20 dogs read as cats.

The more interesting result is the one the accuracy number hides. Grad-CAM puts the model’s attention on the animal’s face for correct predictions, and reading the same maps over its mistakes separates two different kinds of failure. The most confident errors are all cases where the model examined the animal and misread it, including one where it decided on a cat’s collar instead of its face. The lower-confidence errors include cases where attention leaves the animal for outdoor vegetation, and those are consistently cats classified as dogs: the model has partly learned that greenery predicts dog, which holds in this dataset and not in general.

Notebook 03 works through that evidence and what it implies about the accuracy ceiling, which is that the remaining 44 errors are a mixture of unresolvable label noise and two specific, nameable defects rather than a deficit that more training would close.
